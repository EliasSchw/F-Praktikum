\section{Theory}

\subsection{Wave equations}

The propagation of electromagnetic waves in a medium is described by the wave equations:
\begin{align}
    \nabla^2 \vec{E} &= \frac{\mu \varepsilon}{c^2} \frac{\partial^2 \vec{E}}{\partial t^2} + \sigma \mu_0 \mu \frac{\partial \vec{E}}{\partial t} \label{eq:wave_equation_E} \\
    \nabla^2 \vec{H} &= \frac{\mu \varepsilon}{c^2} \frac{\partial^2 \vec{H}}{\partial t^2} + \sigma \mu_0 \mu \frac{\partial \vec{H}}{\partial t} \label{eq:wave_equation_H}
\end{align}
with $\vec{E}$ as the electric field vector, $\vec{H}$ as the magnetic field vector, 
$\mu$ as the permeability, $\mu_0$ as the permeability in vacuum, $\varepsilon$ as the permittivity, $\sigma$ as the conductivity, 
and $c$ as the speed of light in vacuum.
Under the assumption of plane waves, the components $\vec{E} = E_0 e^{i(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}-\omega t)}$ 
and $\vec{H} = H_0 e^{i(\vec{k}\cdot\vec{r}-\omega t)}$ can be used as an ansatz to solve the wave equations \ref{eq:wave_equation_E} and \ref{eq:wave_equation_H}. 
The dispersion relation of the plane waves is then described by:
\begin{align}
    \vec{k}^2 = \omega^2\cdot(\epsilon_0 + i\frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon_0}) \label{eq:dispersion_relation}
\end{align}
with $\vec{k}$ as the wave vector, $\omega$ as the angular frequency, and $\epsilon_0$ as the permittivity of vacuum.

\subsection{Optical properties}
The refractive index $N$:
\begin{align}
    N(\omega) = n(\omega) + i\kappa(\omega) \label{eq:refractive_index}
\end{align}
consists of a real part $n$ and an imaginary part $\kappa$. $\kappa$ is also often referred to as extinction coefficient 
and is related to the dielectric function $\epsilon(\omega)$:
\begin{align}
    \epsilon(\omega) = \epsilon'+i\epsilon'' = N^2 = n^2 - \kappa^2 + 2i n \kappa \label{eq:epsilon}
\end{align}

\subsection{Plane parallel layer}
The investigated semiconductor samples can, in a first approximation, 
be described as a plane parallel layer (see Fig.\,\ref{fig:fabry-perot}) 
with thickness $d$ and refractive index $N$, acting as a Fabry-Pérot interferometer.
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.3\textwidth]{Images/fabry-perot.png}
    \caption{Simplified illustration of the measured samples as a plane parallel plate with thickness $d$ and refractive index $N$. 
    The surrounding air is described with a refractive index $N_\text{air}\approx1$.\cite{Gerthsen}}
    \label{fig:fabry-perot}
\end{figure}
Each incoming beam is partially reflected and partially transmitted at the first interface. In the following
$\alpha$ is set to zero, as only light incoming perpendicular to the surface is considered. Fig.\,\ref{fig:fabry-perot}
shows the incoming light at an angle for better visualization.
The power reflectivity $R_1$ is described by:
\begin{align}
    R_1 = \left\lvert \frac{1 - N}{1 + N} \right\rvert^2 \label{eq:R1}
\end{align}
without considering the interference. As shown in Fig.\,\ref{fig:fabry-perot}, 
the total power reflectivity $R$ consists of reflections of the incoming beam at 
the interfaces of the material. $R$ can be calculated by using a geometric series. 
Additionally, absorption of the material is considered. 
Therefore, $R$ is described by:
\begin{align}
    R = R_{11}+\frac{(1-R_{11})^2R_{12}e^{-2d\beta}}{1-R_{11}R_{12}e^{-2d\beta}}  \label{eq:Rwophase}
\end{align}
with the absorption coefficient
\begin{align}
    \beta = \frac{2\kappa\omega}{c}. \label{eq:beta}
\end{align}
and $R_{11}$ and $R_{12}$ as the reflection coefficients at the first and second interface, respectively.
The total power transmission $T$ can be determined analogously:
\begin{align}
    T = \frac{(1-R_{11})(1-R_{12})e^{-d\beta}}{1-R_{11}R_{12}e^{-2d\beta}}.  \label{eq:T}
\end{align}
Considering the interference,\,\ref{eq:R1} gets more complicated. For a high enough resolution, 
a periodic modulation of the reflectivity dependent on the wave number is expected, 
which results from a relative phase difference between the reflected light see fig.\,\ref{fig:fabry-perot}.
Is the phase difference a multiple of $2\pi$, the light is in phase and the reflectivity is at a maximum.
For a multiple of odd $\pi$, the light is out of phase and the reflectivity is at a minimum.
Considering this, the total power reflectivity $R$ is then described by:
\begin{align}
    R = \left\lvert \frac{(e^{i\frac{2\omega}{c}Nd}-1)(1-N)}{e^{i\frac{2\omega}{c}Nd}(1-N)-(1+N)} \right\rvert^2. \label{eq:R}
\end{align}
The phase shift of the light is described by $e^{i\frac{2\omega}{c}Nd}$. 
The difference between the maxima of the modulation is given by:
\begin{align}
    \Delta \nu = \nu_{\text{m+1}}-\nu_{\text{m}} = \frac{1}{2dN} \label{eq:delta_nu}
\end{align}



\subsection{Drude model}
The Drude model is a classical model in which the electrons in a solid are treated as a gas of free particles. In this model, the dielectric function $\epsilon$ is given by:
\begin{align}
    \epsilon = 1-\frac{\omega^2_\text{p}\tau^2}{1+\omega^2\tau^2}+i\frac{\omega^2_\text{p}\tau}{\omega(1+\omega^2\tau^2)} \label{eq:epsilonDrude}
\end{align}
with the plasma frequency $\omega_\text{p}$:
\begin{align}
    \omega_\text{p} = \sqrt{\frac{N_\text{e}e^2}{\epsilon_0m^*}} \label{eq:omega_p}
\end{align}
and $\tau$ as the relaxation time of the electrons. $m^*$ is the effective mass of the electrons and $N_\text{e}$ the charge carrier density.
At the plasma frequency $\omega_\text{p}$ the real part of the refractive index becomes 
nearly zero $n(\omega_p)\approx0$ and therefore the phase velocity and wavelength diverge. 
This means that all electrons oscillate in phase. Absorption of light goes to zero for 
frequencies below the plasma frequency.
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/theoretical_reflectivity.png}
    \caption{Theoretical reflectivity of a metal according to the Drude model using equations: 
    \ref{eq:R}, \ref{eq:epsilon}, \ref{eq:epsilonDrude}, and \ref{eq:omega_p}. 
    The reflectivity is significantly reduced for wavenumbers above $k_\text{plasma}$.}
    \label{fig:reflectivity}
\end{figure}
Eq.\,\ref{eq:epsilonDrude} can be simplified through the introduction of the high frequency conductivity $\sigma$
\begin{align}
    \sigma = \frac{N_\text{e}e^2\tau}{m^*}\frac{1}{1-i\omega\tau} \label{eq:sigma}
\end{align}
to an expression including the lattice contribution $\epsilon_\text{LC}$ of the material:
\begin{align}
    \epsilon = \epsilon_\text{LC} + i\frac{\sigma}{\omega\epsilon_0}. \label{eq:epsilon_semiconductor}
\end{align}
For metals, the lattice contribution is set to one, $\epsilon_\text{LC}=1$, 
as the electrons are considered a free electron gas.
Using equations \ref{eq:R}, \ref{eq:epsilon}, \ref{eq:epsilonDrude}, and \ref{eq:omega_p}, 
and with the effective mass $m^* = m_\text{e}$ equal to the electron mass, 
the theoretical reflectivity of a metal can be calculated. 
The result is shown in Fig.\,\ref{fig:reflectivity}.
For frequencies below the plasma frequency, the reflectivity is nearly one. 
However, if the frequency of the light is larger than the plasma frequency, the reflectivity is significantly reduced.

\subsection{Semiconductors}\label{sec:semiconductors}
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.45\textwidth]{Images/Bandgap_theory.png}
    \caption{a) Direct and b) indirect semiconductor bandgap. 
    If a photon is absorbed by a valence electron, excitation to the conduction 
    band in the case of an indirect bandgap is only possible with an additional phonon 
    absorption or emission process.\cite{Anleitung}}
    \label{fig:bandgap}
\end{figure}
While metals are well described by the Drude model, the band structure must be considered for semiconductors. 
As shown in Fig.\,\ref{fig:bandgap}, the bandgap can be direct or indirect. 
To ensure energy and momentum conservation, the excitation of a 
valence electron to the conduction band in the case of an indirect bandgap is only possible with an additional 
phonon emission or absorption process.
Electromagnetic waves can polarize the lattice of the semiconductor. 
This leads to a change in the lattice contribution of the dielectric function
\begin{align}
    \epsilon_\text{LC} = \epsilon_\infty\left(1+\frac{\omega^2_\text{LO}-\omega^2_\text{TO}}{\omega^2_\text{TO}-\omega^2-i\Gamma\omega}\right) \label{eq:epsilon_LP}
\end{align}
with $\epsilon_\infty$ as the high-frequency dielectric constant, 
$\omega_\text{LO}$ as the longitudinal optical phonon frequency, 
$\omega_\text{TO}$ as the transverse optical phonon frequency, 
and $\Gamma$ as the damping constant.
Together with the equations \ref{eq:refractive_index} and \ref{eq:epsilon_semiconductor}, 
the theoretical reflectivity of a semiconductor can be calculated, 
as shown in Fig.\,\ref{fig:reflectivity_semiconductor}.

For high frequencies, the characteristics of the bandgap must be considered, as interband excitation processes become possible. For a direct bandgap, the imaginary part of the dielectric function is described by
\begin{align}
    \epsilon^{''}_d = \frac{e^2(2\mu)^{\frac{3}{2}}|p_\text{cv}|^2}{h\epsilon_0m_\text{e}^2(\hbar\omega)^2}\sqrt{\hbar\omega-E_\text{g}} \label{eq:epsilon_d}
\end{align}
and for an indirect bandgap by
\begin{align}
    \epsilon^{''}_\text{id} \propto (\hbar\omega\pm\hbar\Omega_\text{ph}-E_\text{g}) \label{eq:epsilon_id}
\end{align}
for $\hbar\omega \geq E_\text{g}\pm\hbar\Omega$. $p_\text{cv}$ is the pulse matrix element of the interband transition, $\mu$ is the reduced mass of the electron-hole pair, $E_\text{g}$ is the bandgap energy, $\Omega_\text{ph}$ is the phonon resonance frequency, and $m_\text{e}$ is the electron mass.
