\subsection{Signal-to-Noise Ratio}
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/SignalToNoise.png}
    \caption{Transmission measurements for $N_\text{scan} = 10$ and $N_\text{scan} = 100$ mirror passes. The inset shows the part used for the evaluation of the signal-to-noise ratio (see Fig.\,\ref{fig:StNlog}).}
    \label{fig:StN}
\end{figure}
To determine the influence of the number of scans, $N_\text{scan}$, 
on the signal-to-noise ratio, measurements with a resolution of $4\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ were performed at 
various values of $N_\text{scan}$. These are transmission measurements with ambient air. 
A region with generally less noise (see Fig.\,\ref{fig:StN}) and no gas absorption 
(see Fig.\,\ref{fig:GasAbsorption}) was identified between $6000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ and $7000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$. 
To quantitatively examine the behavior of the signal-to-noise ratio, 
the mean value $T_\text{mean}$ is divided by the standard deviation $T_\text{std}$ for each 
transmission measurement. Thus, the signal-to-noise ratio $SNR$ is given by:
\begin{align}
    SNR = \frac{T_\text{mean}}{T_\text{std}}
\end{align}
Theoretically, one expects the $SNR$ to grow according to:
\begin{align}
    SNR = N^{a}
\end{align}
with $a = 0.5$ \cite{Script}. To check this dependency, 
the signal-to-noise ratio is plotted for various values of $N$ in a double logarithmic scale.
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/STNLog.png}
    \caption{$SNR$ versus the number of mirror passes in double 
    logarithmic scaling using the spectral range between $6000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ and $7000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$.}
    \label{fig:StNlog}
\end{figure}
From the slope of the linear regression, $a$ can be determined as:
\begin{align}
    a = \stn
\end{align}
which matches the expectation.

\subsection{Reflection and transmission at low resolution}\label{sec:RTlowRes}
The reflection and transmission spectra shown in fig.\,\ref{fig:highResRef} and \ref{fig:highResTrans} 
are measured with a lower resolution of $4\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ than in \ref{sec:RTlowRes} 
but with the same number of mirror passes ($N=50$) to save measurement time. 
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/Low_Res_Reflections.png}
    \caption{Normalized reflection spectra of all semiconductor samples. The spectra are slightly smoothened.}
    \label{fig:lowResRef}

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/Low_Res_Transmissions.png}
    \caption{Normalized transmission spectra of all semiconductor samples. The spectra are slightly smoothened.}
    \label{fig:lowResTrans}
\end{figure}
Both the transmission and reflection spectra behave as expected. As the band gap of the materials is approached, 
transmission and reflection drop to near zero. This happens because the absorption increases as the light has enough 
energy to excite the electrons from the valence band to the conduction band.
The transmission of the doped Si sample is barely detectable and is close to zero over the entire spectral range. 
This is due to the high doping concentration which leads to an increase in the plasma frequency.
As a result, the sample behaves more like a metal than a semiconductor.

\subsection{Bandgap and pulse matrix element}
\subsubsection{Direct semiconductor}
The band gap $E_\text{g}$ and the pulse matrix element $|p_\text{cv}|$ can be evaluated using eq.\,\ref{eq:epsilon_d}.
If $(\epsilon''^2\omega^2)^2$ is plotted against the wave number $k$, a linear regime around the bandgap can be found.
The dependence given in eq.\,\ref{epsilon_d} allows the bandgap to be determined by extrapolating the linear regime to $(\epsilon''^2\omega^2)^2=0$ to obtain the bandgap.
$ p_\text{cv}|$ is given by the slope of the extrapolation.
The method of obtaining the bandgap and the pulse matrix element is shown in fig.\,\ref{fig:GaAsDopedBandGap}.
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/GaAs Doped.png}
    \caption{Determination of the bandgap and pulse matrix element for a doped GaAs sample.}
    \label{fig:GaAsDopedBandGap}
\end{figure}
The interband excitation can clearly be seen and follows an exponential behaviour. This behaviour can be drescribed
with the phenomenon of the Urbach tail \cite{Script}
The determined values for the given direct semiconductor samples are shown in tab.\,\ref{tab:directBandgapPulseMatrixElement}.
The reduced mass $\mu$ is calculated by using eq:\ref{eq:reduced_mass}:
\begin{align}
    \mu = (\frac{1}{m^*_\text{e}}+\frac{1}{m^*_\text{hh}})^{-1} \label{eq:reduced_mass}
\end{align}
with the effective masses $m^*_\text{e}$ and $m^*_\text{hh}$ for the conduction band and heavy hole band respectively.
For GaAs and GaSb the effective masses are given in tab.\,\ref{tab:effectiveMasses}.

    \end{tabular}

\subsection{Indirect semiconductor}
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/Si_Undoped.png}
    \caption{Determination of the bandgap and pulse matrix element for a undoped Si sample.}
    \label{fig:SiUndopedBandGap}
\end{figure}
For indirect semiconductors, an additional phonon is required for a electron to be excited form the valence
band to the conduction band (see \ref{sec:semiconductors}).
The bandgap can be determined by using eq.\,\ref{eq:epsilon_id}, by plotting $\sqrt{\beta\omega^2}$ over the wavenumber.
This time, two lineare regimes should be found, which can be seen in the equation. When extrapolating
both linear regimes to $\sqrt{\beta\omega^2}=0$, intercepts for $E_\text{g}-\hbar\Omega_\text{ph}$
and $E_\text{g}+\hbar\Omega_\text{ph}$ emerge. Adding both intercepts and deviding by two gives the bandgap,
whereas the difference of both intercepts divided by two gives the phonon energy $\hbar\Omega_\text{ph}$.
An example for the extrapolation is given in fig.\,\ref{fig:SiUndopedBandGap}.
The determined values for the given indirect semiconductor samples are shown in tab.\,\ref{tab:indirectBandgapPulseMatrixElement}.
