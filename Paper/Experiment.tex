\subsection{Signal to noise ratio}
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/SignalToNoise.png}
    \caption{Transmission measurements for $N_\text{scan}=10$ and $N_\text{scan}=100$ mirror passes.
    The inset shows the part used for evaluation of the signal to noise ratio (see fig.\,\ref{fig:StNlog})}
    \label{fig:StN}
\end{figure}
To determine the influence of the number of scans $N_\text{scan}$ on the signal to noise ratio, measurements with a resolution
of $4\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ are done at various $N_\text{scan}$. Those are transmission measurements with ambient air.
Between $6000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ and $7000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ a region with generally less noise (see fig.\,\ref{fig:StN})
and no gas absorption (see fig.\,\ref{fig:GasAbsorption}) is identified. To quantitatively examine the
behaviour of the signal to noise ratio, the mean value $T_\text{mean}$ is divided by the Standard deviation
$T_\text{std}$ for every transmission measurement. 
Thus the signal to noise ratio $SNR$ is given by:
\begin{align}
    SNR = \frac{T_\text{mean}}{T_\text{std}}
\end{align}
Theoretically, one expects $SNR$ to grow according to:
\begin{align}
    SNR = N^{a}
\end{align}
with $a=0.5$ \cite{Script}. To check this dependency, the signal
to noise ratio is plottet for various N's in a double logarithmic scale.
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/STNLog.png}
    \caption{$SNR$ over the number of mirror passes in double logarithmic scaling using the specral range between
    $6000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ and $7000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$.}
    \label{fig:StNlog}
\end{figure}
From the slope of the linear regression $a$ can be determined as:
\begin{align}
    a = \stn 
\end{align} 
which matches with the expectation.

\subsection{Bandgap and pulse matrix element}
\subsubsection{Direct semiconductor}
The band gap $E_\text{g}$ and pulse matrix element $|p_\text{cv}|$ can be evaluated by using eq.\,\ref{eq:epsilon_d}.
When plotting $(\epsilon''^2\omega^2)^2$ over the wave number $k$ a linear regime around the bandgap can be found.
The dependency given in eq.\,\ref{epsilon_d} allows the determination of the bandgap by extrapolating the linear regime to
$(\epsilon''^2\omega^2)^2=0$ to obtain the bandgap. $|p_\text{cv}|$ is given by the slope of the extrapolation.
The method of obtaining the bandgap and pulse matrix element is shown in fig.\,\ref{fig:GaAsDopedBandGap}.
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/GaAs Doped.png}
    \caption{Determination of the bandgap and pulse matrix element for a doped GsAs sample.}
    \label{fig:GaAsDopedBandGap}
\end{figure}
The determined values for the given direct semiconductor samples are shown in tab.\,\ref{tab:directBandgapPulseMatrixElement}.
The reduced mass $\mu$ is calculated by using eq:\ref{eq:reduced_mass}:
\begin{align}
    \mu = (\frac{1}{m^*_\text{e}}+\frac{1}{m^*_\text{hh}})^{-1} \label{eq:reduced_mass}
\end{align}
with the effective masses $m^*_\text{e}$ and $m^*_\text{hh}$ for the conduction band and heavy hole band respectively.

\subsection{Indirect semiconductor}
For indirect semiconductors, an additional phonon is required for a electron to be excited form the valence
band to the conduction band (see \ref{sec:semiconductors}).
The bandgap can be determined by using eq.\,\ref{eq:epsilon_id}, by plotting $\sqrt{\beta\omega^2}$ over the wavenumber.
This time, two lineare regimes should be found, which can be seen in the equation. When extrapolating
both linear regimes to $\sqrt{\beta\omega^2}=0$, intercepts for $E_\text{g}-\hbar\Omega_\text{ph}$
and $E_\text{g}+\hbar\Omega_\text{ph}$ emerge. Adding both intercepts and deviding by two gives the bandgap,
whereas the difference of both intercepts divided by two gives the phonon energy $\hbar\Omega_\text{ph}$.
An example for the extrapolation is given in fig.\,\ref{fig:SiUndopedBandGap}.
