To identify the spectral ranges where the results are influenced by gas absorption, 
a measurement in ambient air with a resolution of $0.3\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ and $N = 10$ scans was performed.
It is important to know how much the gas absorption influences the measurement,
as this can lead to significant errors in the evaluation of the optical components. 
In fig.\,\ref{fig:GasAbsorptionSample} the gas absorption dips are shown. To evaluate the influence of the
different air molecules, the normalized transmission spectra are used.
The measurement in air was normalized with the corresponding reference measurement, which was carried out 
under vacuum. Gas absorption dips are expected at frequencies corresponding to the resonance frequencies 
of the air molecules. The wavenumbers for the measured gas absorption are shown in Tab.\,\ref{tab:gasAbsorption}.
All the measured data agree with the literature values for the gases $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{CO}_2$.

\subsection{Signal-to-Noise Ratio}
To determine the influence of the number of scans, $N_\text{scan}$, 
on the signal-to-noise ratio, measurements with a resolution of $4\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ were performed at 
various values of $N_\text{scan}$. These are transmission measurements with ambient air. 
A region with generally less noise (see Fig.\,\ref{fig:StN}) and low gas absorption 
between $6000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ and $7000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ was picked. 
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/SignalToNoise.png}
    \caption{Transmission measurements for $N_\text{scan} = 10$ and $N_\text{scan} = 100$ mirror passes. 
    The inset shows the part used for the evaluation of the signal-to-noise ratio (see Fig.\,\ref{fig:StNlog}).}
    \label{fig:StN}
\end{figure}
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/STNLog.png}
    \caption{$SNR$ versus the number of mirror passes in double 
    logarithmic scaling using the spectral range between $6000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ and $7000\,\text{cm}^{-1}$.}
    \label{fig:StNlog}
\end{figure}
To quantitatively examine the behavior of the signal-to-noise ratio, 
the mean value $T_\text{mean}$ is divided by the standard deviation $T_\text{std}$ for each 
transmission measurement. Thus, the signal-to-noise ratio $SNR$ is given by:
\begin{align}
    SNR = \frac{T_\text{mean}}{T_\text{std}}
\end{align}
Theoretically, one expects the $SNR$ to grow according to:
\begin{align}
    SNR = N_\text{scan}^{a}
\end{align}
with $a = 0.5$ \cite{Script}. To check this dependency, 
the signal-to-noise ratio is plotted for various values of $N_\text{scan}$ in a double logarithmic scale.
From the slope of the linear regression, $a$ can be determined as:
\begin{align}
    a = \stn
\end{align}
which matches the expectation.

The transmission spectra shown in fig.\,\ref{fig:highResTransNu} are measured with a resolution of 
$0.3\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ and $N_\text{scan}=50$ mirror passes. The aim of this precise measurement is to 
determine the distance between the oscillations seen in the inset of the fig.\,\ref{fig:highResTransNu}.
Using equation \ref{eq:delta_nu} the refractive Index $N$ can be calculated.
Additionally, the dielectric permittivity can be calculated using $\kappa$ from the low resolution data
according to eq.\,\ref{eq:epsilon}. In the spectral range where $n$ could be determined using the oscillations, $\kappa$ nearly vanishes
and therefore $\epsilon''$ vanishes too. $\epsilon'$ is shown in fig.\,\ref{fig:epsilonoscillation}.
In the observed spectral range, $\epsilon'$ is increasing with higher wavenumbers for the three samples. 
The distance between the oscillations is averaged over the spectral range of $100\,\text{cm}^{-1}$
in positive and negative direction from the assigned wavenumber (contains about $50$ to $70$ oscillations) and measured between the corresponding peaks
like shown in fig.\,\ref{fig:peaks}.
Peak detection is only possible for spectral ranges with low noise. Fig.\,\ref{fig:peaks} shows a spectral range
with average noise, where it is possible to detect the peaks.
The method is done for different wavenumbers shown in fig.\,\ref{fig:Eliasistzulangsam}
For the doped GaSb sample, determination of the refractive index was not possible, as the signal was too noisy.
Furthermore, the refractive index of the other samples could also only be determined in a limited, less noisy spectral range.
The most significant contribution to the error is the sample thickness, which might differ from the given value. 
The error is assumed to be $2\%$.
Negligible influences on the error are, for example, 
the picket fence effect or the change in wavelength over the spectral range.

    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/Low_Res_Transmissions.png}
    \caption{Normalized transmission spectra of all semiconductor samples. The spectra are slightly smoothened.}
    \label{fig:lowResTrans}

The reflection and transmission spectra shown in fig.\,\ref{fig:lowResRef} and \ref{fig:lowResTrans} 
are measured with a lower resolution of $4\,\text{cm}^{-1}$ than in \ref{sec:RTlowRes} 
but with the same number of mirror passes ($N_\text{scan}=50$) to save measurement time.
Smoothening of the spectra is done by using exponential smoothening.
As the band gap of the materials is approached, 
transmittivity and reflection show a significant drop. The transmittivity drops even futher nerly to zero.
This happens because the absorption increases as the light has enough 
energy to excite the electrons from the valence band to the conduction band.
The transmission of the doped Si sample is barely detectable and is close to zero over the entire spectral range. 
This is due to the high doping concentration which leads to an increase in the plasma frequency.
As a result, the sample behaves more like a metal than a semiconductor.

\subsection{Bandgap and pulse matrix element}
The obtained data of fig.\,\ref{fig:n} can be compared with the data obtained from the high resolution measurement and evaluation of the oscillations in fig.\,\ref{fig:Eliasistzulangsam}:
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/RefractiveIndicesVergleich.png}
    \caption{Comparison of the refractive index $n$ determined from the oscillations of the high resolution data and obtained from the low resolution data of transmittivity and reflection.}
    \label{fig:comparison}

One can see that the refractive indices differ slightly from each other. This is due to the fact that different datasets are used for the evaluation of $n$.
For the high resolution data, only the transmittivity is used, whereas for the low resolution data both transmittivity and reflectivity are used to determine $n$.
Furthermore, for the high resolution method, the mean distance of the oscillations is used for a wide spectral range, which can also cause some deviation.
However, most datapoints for the Si and doped GaAs sample are in agreement within their error with each other. $n$ for GaAs undoped differs more significantly.

\subsubsection{Direct semiconductor}
The band gap $E_\text{g}$ and the absolute value of the pulse matrix element $|p_\text{cv}|$ can be evaluated using eq.\,\ref{eq:epsilon_d}.
If $(\epsilon''^2\omega^2)^2$ is plotted against the wave number $\nu$, a linear regime around the bandgap can be found.
The dependence given in eq.\,\ref{eq:epsilon_d} allows the bandgap to be determined by extrapolating the linear regime to $(\epsilon''^2\omega^2)^2=0$ to obtain the bandgap.
$|p_\text{cv}|$ is given up to a factor by the slope of the extrapolation.
The method of obtaining the bandgap and the pulse matrix element is shown in fig.\,\ref{fig:GaAsDopedBandGap} with the region for linear regrssion marked.
\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{Images/GaAs Doped.png}
    \caption{Determination of the bandgap and pulse matrix element for a doped GaAs sample (smoothened).}
    \label{fig:GaAsDopedBandGap}
\end{figure}
The plots for the undoped GaAs and the doped GaSb sample are shown sec.\,\ref{sec:appendix}
The interband excitation is clearly visible and follows an exponential behaviour. This behaviour can be described by the Urbach tail phenomenon \cite{Script}.
The values determined for the given direct semiconductor samples are shown in tab.\,\ref{tab:directBandgapPulseMatrixElement}.

\end{align}
with the effective masses $m^*_\text{e}$ and $m^*_\text{hh}$ for the conduction band and heavy hole band respectively.

\subsection{Indirect semiconductor}
\begin{figure}[h]

For indirect semiconductors, an additional phonon is required to excite an electron from 
the valence band to the conduction band (see \ref{sec:semiconductors}).
The band gap can be determined using eq.\,\ref{eq:epsilon_id} by plotting $\sqrt{\beta\omega^2}$ 
over the wavenumber.
This time we should find two linear regimes, as shown in the equation. Extrapolating both linear 
regimes to $\sqrt{\beta\omega^2}=0$ gives intercepts for $E_\text{g}-\hbar\Omega_\text{ph}$ and $E_\text{g}+\hbar\Omega_\text{ph}$. 
The mean intercept gives the bandgap, while the difference gives twice the phonon 
energy $\hbar\Omega_\text{ph}$.
The extrapolation for undoped Si is given in fig.\,\ref{fig:SiUndopedBandGap}.
The value obtained for the given Si sample are shown in 
tab.\,\ref{tab:indirectBandgapPulseMatrixElement}.
